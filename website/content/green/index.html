We tried to buy the good life. It cost the earth — and we're lonelier than ever.

Look at a normal household: a drill used for fourteen minutes across its entire life, a garage you can't park in, closets and a storage unit and a landfill filling up downstream — and somehow fewer real friends than our parents had. The engine overheating the planet is the same one that sold us stuff in place of each other.

You can keep optimizing the buy-it, use-it-once, throw-it-out loop and shave a few percent off the footprint, or you can change what people reach for in the first place. Relationships are the one resource that compounds when you spend it, so that's where we build: moving people from owning to borrowing, from buying to sharing, from piling up stuff to spending time together. Every gathering Ripls sparks and every tool it keeps in circulation takes a little less from the planet and gives a little more back to each other.

One sentence — and buying stops being the default.

Someone types "camping trip, three weeks out" or "fence build Saturday," and out comes a real invite with the gear list filled in from what the group already owns — the tent, the truck, the chop saw, the folding tables. Nobody drives to the store, because the same handful of people lend instead of buy and share instead of own.

Small stuff, but small stuff multiplied across a neighborhood and repeated every weekend bends a consumption curve. And it isn't a mockup — Ripls is a working app, in private beta with real groups in a handful of towns, where borrow-don't-buy is already the default rather than the lecture.

Avoided carbon you can actually count.

Every loan, every giveaway, every shared meal throws off a number — the purchase that didn't happen, the embodied carbon avoided, the waste kept out of the landfill. We've tracked it from day one, even while it's small, because for a climate fund that's the asset: impact that's counted, not claimed.

The per-ton story also gets cheaper as the network thickens. The relationships driving the sharing are the cheapest acquisition there is — a friend pulling in a friend rather than an ad pushing a stranger — so the carbon math and the growth math improve together.

A circular economy that doesn't need a subsidy.

The last wave of sharing apps died because they were transactional — strangers, deposits, friction, no trust. Ripls runs on the crew you already have, so the trust comes free and the sharing sticks. When a group moves money through it — splitting a cabin, pooling for a fence — we keep a small slice the way any payment processor does, and the clubs, leagues, and teams that run this every week pay a flat monthly rate for the heavier tools.

There are no ads and no data sold, and the greener it runs — more borrowed, less bought — the more it earns. The margin and the mission finally pull the same way, which is the thing a decade of well-meaning green apps never quite managed.

Degrowth polls terribly. This is the version people actually want.

Telling people to consume less reads as sacrifice, and sacrifice loses every time. But nobody really wanted the stuff — they wanted what it was supposed to buy: status, security, a sense of belonging. Hand them the belonging directly and the lower-carbon life tags along on its own, because it happens to be the better one.

That's the piece the climate movement has been missing — the demand-side product that doesn't feel like a punishment, the one that makes the low-consumption life what people pick on a Friday night. The hard part of climate was never the panels or the batteries; it's getting billions of people to want a different life. Reweave the social fabric and the consumption curve bends with it — not because anyone gave anything up, but because they finally got the thing they were buying all that stuff to chase.
