Everyone said "let me know if you need anything." The hard part can be knowing how to ask.

Every mom has a list of things that could really help: Twenty minutes to shower without holding her on your hip. A walk where the other person responds in words. The 6–9 month onesies from the neighbor whose kid outgrew them. A friend going through the witching hour at the same time you are.

The village exists. It said let me know. But asking is its own job — wording the text, choosing who to ask, worrying about imposing, walking back the request after a "we're slammed too" reply, then remembering the thank-you and tracking down whose casserole pan you still have.

You don't have a spare job to spare. So the help stays theoretical, and you handle it alone.

Tell it what you need
in one line.

"Need a meal for tonight — baby's been up since 4am, and I'm exhausted." Out comes the full request. Day, time, what you actually need, dietary notes if you have them, a no-pressure tone, even a "soup or a frozen lasagna both fine" line that takes the perfectionism out. Drop in a Doordash gift card if you want — done in seconds.

if anyone said yes.

Who's bringing Tuesday's dinner. Who's grabbing the hand-me-downs. Who's coming for the walk.

List what you need — Tuesday lasagna (no garlic, breastfeeding), the 6–9 month onesies Sarah said she'd pass on, a friend to walk to the coffee place at 2pm, somebody to hold the baby for twenty minutes Saturday morning. Friends and neighbors tap what they've got. No mass-texting, no awkward asking. The list fills in real time and everybody can see it.

Why buy the playmat
your friend already outgrew?

One tap to ask, one tap to offer — pickup time and porch address baked in, no group-chat back-and-forth. The Snoo. The bouncer. The bottle warmer. The high chair you'll need in three months. Most baby gear gets used for eight weeks and stored for a decade — in a working circle, all of it moves. No village needs to buy ten of the same thing.

Every meal train ends with the next one half-planned.

After the train wraps, a short note thanks everyone and asks the question — was that helpful? want to do it monthly? A pre-drafted next instance shows up: same circle, next month, copy from what worked. You approve. You don't recreate.

And when you've been the one organizing the playgroup for three months straight, the system finds the next mom up and sends a one-tap "want to host the next one?" Somebody steps up, because nobody had to remember to ask.

Five moms, a meal train, and somebody actually sleeps.

A Thursday in October. A friend in the circle posts a sentence: toddler has hand-foot-mouth, partner traveling, deeply not okay. By Friday morning the calendar's full — three meals scheduled, two friends coming for the afternoon, somebody's sister-in-law drops off a brand-new puzzle the kid hasn't seen. Nobody had to ask twice. Nobody had to text the same line to nine people.

It used to happen this way without an app. The muscle's still there. The choreography came back the moment somebody made it easy to ask.

Your circle is private. Period.

Your address, your kid's drop-off time, your routine, the messages you've sent — visible to the people you invited, invisible to anyone else. We don't show ads. We don't sell data. We don't recommend you to strangers. We're not a Facebook Group with the search bar removed.

Built by parents who got tired of every other version of this — the Slack channel that got messy, the group text that lost the thread, the neighborhood app that turned dark. We made the thing we wanted: a closed circle that runs quietly enough that the only thing you notice is that the help arrives.
